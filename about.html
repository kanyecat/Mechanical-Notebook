About

A notebook of moving parts

Mechanical Notebook is a free, open-source place to see how machines move, play with them, and learn the math behind them.

Why I made it

I love finding out how things move. A gear train, a cam or a linkage can turn one simple motion into something surprising, and once you see how it works you start noticing it everywhere: in clocks, toys, bicycles, engines and even in animals.

Most references show these mechanisms as still drawings, or as finished animations you can only watch. I wanted a notebook where you can change a length or a tooth count and immediately see what happens, then check the numbers yourself. When an idea is ready to build, the generators turn it into files for a laser cutter, a CNC machine or a 3D printer.

Who it is for

  • Anyone curious about how movements are created, with no engineering background needed. Hover the underlined words for plain-language definitions.
  • Students who want to see the math come alive, with worked practice problems and design challenges.
  • Makers, artists and designers building kinetic sculptures, automata, robots and prototypes.
  • Engineers who want a quick, free tool to sketch a mechanism, check it and export it.

Who made it

Hi, I'm Joo Hee Kim. I studied at Carnegie Mellon University, where I earned a Bachelor of Engineering Studies and Arts (BESA) in Mechanical Engineering and Art, with a minor in Physical Computing.

Mechanical Notebook brings those two sides together: the precision of engineering and the clear, hands-on way an artist explains things.

How it is made

Every drawing is calculated live in your browser with plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. There are no accounts and nothing to install. The code is open source under the MIT licence, so you are free to learn from it, use it and improve it.

The site is inspired by Henry T. Brown's 507 Mechanical Movements (1868). All explanations and drawings here are original.
